How do I get started with using Bitwarden for password management
To get started with Bitwarden, you'll need to 
create a secure account, install the necessary applications on your devices, and begin populating your encrypted vault with login credentials. 
1. Create Your Account
Sign Up: Visit the Bitwarden registration page to create an account using your email address.
Set a Master Password: This is the only password you need to remember. It should be long, unique, and strong. Consider using a passphrase—a string of random words that is easy for you to remember but hard for computers to guess.
Verify Your Email: Check your inbox for a verification link to activate your account and gain access to the Web Vault.

2. Install Bitwarden on Your Devices
To get the most out of Bitwarden, download the apps from the official download page for all your frequently used devices: 
Browser Extension: Available for Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge, and more. This is the most efficient way to autofill passwords while browsing.
Mobile App: Install on iOS or Android to access your passwords on the go.
Desktop App: A standalone application for managing your vault directly from your computer.

3. Secure Your Vault
Enable Two-Step Login (2FA): Add an extra layer of security by requiring a second form of identification, such as a code from an authenticator app (e.g., Bitwarden Authenticator, Authy, or Google Authenticator).
Record Your Recovery Code: Once 2FA is enabled, Bitwarden provides a recovery code. Save this in a safe, offline location (like a physical safe) in case you lose access to your 2FA device.
Disable Browser Password Managers: To avoid conflicts, disable the built-in password saving features in your web browsers.

Import Data: If you are switching from another password manager, you can import your existing passwords directly into Bitwarden.

You’ve got a very solid handle on the core concepts. Here is the breakdown to address those specific concerns without the fluff:
1. Data Breaches & The "Master Key"
Your understanding is mostly correct. Bitwarden uses salted hashing and AES-256 bit encryption.
The Key: When you log in, your Master Password is transformed into an encryption key locally on your device. Bitwarden never sees or stores your actual password.
The Breach Scenario: If a hacker steals Bitwarden’s entire database, they get a pile of "unreadable gibberish." Without your Master Password (which only exists in your head), they cannot unlock your data.
Google/Apple: The risk isn't usually a breach of Google/Apple themselves, but rather session hijacking. if a hacker gets into your Chrome browser or your unlocked iPhone, they often have "path of least resistance" access to your saved passwords because the "gate" is just your device passcode or Google login.
2. Platform Bias
Bitwarden avoids bias because it was built using cross-platform frameworks (like Xamarin and Electron) rather than native Apple or Windows code.
Performance: It works essentially the same everywhere. However, the Browser Extension is generally considered the "best" version because it’s where most people do their logging in.
The Trade-off: Because it isn't "native," it might not feel as "silky smooth" as Apple’s keychain, but it is functionally identical across Windows, Mac, Linux, iOS, and Android.
3. The Open-Source "Risk"
This is a classic debate, but the consensus is that open-source is safer.
Security by Obscurity: Proprietary code (Google/Apple) relies on "hiding" the locks. Open-source assumes the hacker has the blueprints but makes the "lock" so mathematically strong it doesn't matter.
The "Audit" Reality: You’re right—you won't audit it. But security firms and "bug bounty" hunters do it for a living. They find and report holes before bad actors can weaponize them. In closed-source, a hole could exist for years without anyone knowing but the hackers.
4. Storing Sensitive Files (Photos/Videos)
Bitwarden allows File Attachments, but it is not optimized for a "media library."
Security: Yes, a video stored in a Bitwarden "Secure Note" is incredibly safe from leaks because of that zero-knowledge encryption.
The Practicality Problem: Free accounts have limited storage. Even with a Premium account (1GB), video files are huge and slow to upload/download because the app has to encrypt/decrypt the whole file every time you touch it.
The Verdict: Great for a "racy photo" or a scan of a social security card; terrible for a 4K home movie collection. For large media, look into "zero-knowledge" cloud storage like Proton Drive or Ente.

Not right now. Thanks. I do have a follow-up question on 1. Why can't the encrypted passwords just be decrypted?
It's because the "key" to the lock isn't stored in the building.
To decrypt your vault, a hacker would need two things: the encrypted data (which they might get in a breach) and your Master Password (which they don't have).
Here’s why they can't just "crack" it:
Mathematical Wall: Bitwarden uses AES-256 bit encryption. This is the same standard used by governments for top-secret data. To "brute force" it—meaning a computer tries every possible combination of characters—would take trillions of years with current technology.
No "Reset" Button: Unlike your bank or Gmail, Bitwarden has no "I forgot my password" link that sends you a temporary code. If you lose your Master Password, your data is gone forever. This is because Bitwarden literally doesn't have the key to reset it for you.
Local Decryption: The "unscrambling" happens only on your device (phone or laptop) using your Master Password as the catalyst. By the time your passwords leave your phone to sync to Bitwarden’s servers, they are already scrambled into gibberish.
The only real way a hacker gets in is if they guess your Master Password (because it's weak) or steal it from you directly (using a phishing link or a keylogger on your computer).
